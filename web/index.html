Coastal floods come far more often than they used to. They still last about two hours.

Tide gauges along the US coast record when the sea rises above the local flood line. NOAA publishes how many days that happens — not how long the water stays up. This measures that, at 137 gauges across every year on record.

Every station

Recent 10-year averages, sorted by hours per flood day. years is usable years; * marks records too short for a trend claim.

Why this exists. Across these 137 gauges a flood day means anything from just over an hour above the threshold to most of a day — and NOAA's published counts score every one of them the same. NOAA's Inundation Analysis Tool can compute duration, one station and a limited date range at a time. What has not existed is the bulk history: duration made comparable across thousands of station-years alongside the published day counts. That is what this is.

The pattern behind the headline. Flood days rose at 73 of the 86 gauges with long enough records to compare their first and last decade. Over the same spans the length of an individual flood barely moved — a median change of about +0.01 hours per decade across the 41 longest records, with 28 of those 41 flat to within a quarter of their starting value. More floods, the same shape.
